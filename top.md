# Choices
What if you could (should) only purchase the minimal kit to get out on the water? What would that be and why? Is this even possible, given the mix of conditions and skills kayakers need? Perhaps it's purchasing a 'quiver killer' or 'Swiss-army knife' type of boat, e.g., a boat that does it all well, but likely doesn't excel at any particular condition; what would you pick? For this list, we've hedged a bit by offering some options, e.g., a composite boat and a plastic boat, although you could argue that plastic boats tend to be better all-rounders and require less care.

There you have it. Realistically, about £2,500-3,000 to get started minimally, or much more if you want to spend more.

The kit list assumes you will wear fleece, nylon, or merino undergarments under a drysuit. For the car rack, you can go much, much higher: Thule Hullavator Pro costs £1,200 for 2 boats, or full rack lift systems cost £800-1,500. The Thule high is based on 2 x Hullavator Pro to carry 2 boats, plus the racks and fittings. The same goes for other high-end items; e.g., Gore-Tex drysuits cost > £1,500. Some items quoted above are on sale or perpetually on sale, and you could substantially reduce the cost by buying some things, especially the boat, used. Although boats seem to keep a fair amount of value, especially glass/carbon boats, used plastic boats typically cost around £500, and glass boats more than £ 1,500.

Note the radio difference is VHF radio vs. VHF with AIS and DSC.

For obvious reasons, you probably shouldn't buy some items used, e.g., buoyancy aids.

One major caveat: Just because something is carbon doesn't mean it's great! Carbon is always sexy, superlight, and expensive. It is almost never durable and, despite being super stiff, will break in weak areas. You could spend £400-500 on a carbon Greenland paddle or make your own wooden one for £50-100, and I doubt the weight difference will be that significant.
